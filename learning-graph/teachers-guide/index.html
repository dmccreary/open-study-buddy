    Teacher's Guide¶

    This guide is for teachers, club leaders and mentors who run Open Study Buddy with a group of students. It explains every part of the book, how to use it in class, how to run a classroom that builds a device with a microphone in it, and how to make your own copy. The Purchasing Guide at the end lists every part to order. No technical background is assumed: each technical term is defined the first time it appears.

    About This Textbook¶

    What Is an Intelligent Textbook?¶

    An intelligent textbook is a digital textbook that goes beyond text and pictures. It adds interactive simulations, a searchable glossary, answers to common questions, and a map of how the concepts depend on each other.

    The Five Levels of Intelligent Textbooks¶

    Intelligent textbooks are grouped into five levels by how interactive and adaptive they are:

    Level Name Description Example features
    Level 1 Static Digital A PDF or basic web version of a print textbook Text and images only
    Level 2 Interactive Adds simulations, self-checks and a searchable glossary MicroSims, self-check questions, search
    Level 3 Adaptive Adjusts content to each student's performance Personalized learning paths
    Level 4 AI-Assisted Adds an AI tutor that answers student questions Chatbot, automated feedback
    Level 5 Fully Adaptive AI Learns from student interactions and adapts continuously Real-time content generation

    This book is a Level 2 intelligent textbook. The device students build is a personal AI agent, but the book itself does not track students or adapt to them.

    What Makes This Book Different¶

    • Students build a real device. Over 18 chapters, students write every line of MicroPython for a desk companion that listens, shows what it heard on a round display, speaks answers aloud, and announces tests and due dates from the school calendar.
    • It is open from end to end. The school, not a vendor, decides where the agent behind the device runs and which data it may touch. There is no subscription.
    • It teaches responsible listening. A device with a microphone in a classroom raises real privacy questions, and the book treats them as part of the engineering (Chapter 17).
    • 45 MicroSims let students explore ideas such as sample rate, FFT bins and frame budgets in the browser, with no software to install.
    • A learning graph maps how all 326 concepts in the book build on each other.
    • Sage the Owl, a friendly guide character, appears in every chapter with tips, warnings and encouragement.
    • It is free. The book is licensed under Creative Commons for non-commercial use.

    Before You Start¶

    Who the Book Is For¶

    The main audience is high school students in grades 9–12. Motivated middle school students, college undergraduates, coding clubs and hobbyists can also use it.

    Students should already have finished the Learning MicroPython course or have the same experience. That means they can put MicroPython on a Raspberry Pi Pico, use Thonny, write loops and functions, read a button and drive a display. Chapter 2 is a refresher, not a first introduction. No signal-processing, networking or AI experience is needed.

    What You Need¶

    Item When it is first needed Notes
    One Study Buddy base kit per student or pair Chapter 4 See the Purchasing Guide
    A computer per student or pair with Thonny installed Chapter 2 Windows, Mac, Linux or a Raspberry Pi all work
    A Micro-USB data cable per kit Chapter 2 Charge-only cables look the same but cannot program the Pico
    A Wi-Fi network the Pico can join Chapter 13 See the network note below
    A Raspberry Pi or computer to run the class server Chapters 15–16 Runs the MQTT broker and the buddy server
    Soldering iron, wire strippers, small screwdriver Before Chapter 4 Only if your microphone and amplifier boards come without headers soldered on

    Check your school network early

    The Pico 2 W joins ordinary password-protected Wi-Fi networks. Many school networks need a sign-in page or a separate login for each user, and a Pico cannot get past either. Before Chapter 13, ask your technology staff for a network the devices can join, or run a small classroom router that is not connected to the internet.

    Using the Chapters¶

    Chapter Sequence¶

    The book has 18 chapters. Each chapter adds one idea and ends with a working device feature, so students should work through them in order.

    Chapters Topic area
    1–2 Foundations: personal agents, the Study Buddy architecture, a MicroPython refresher
    3–4 Sound basics and the kit hardware: sampling, wiring all five parts, power and ground
    5–7 Display and buttons: pixels and color, redrawing only what changed, debouncing, device modes
    8–12 Audio and signal processing: I2S recording and playback, the FFT, fast MicroPython, push-to-talk, wake words
    13–16 Networking: Wi-Fi and HTTP, streaming and security, MQTT alerts, the school agent and calendar
    17–18 Responsibility and integration: privacy and consent, testing, capstone projects

    What Each Chapter Contains¶

    1. Summary: what the chapter covers and what students will build.
    2. Concepts Covered: the concepts from the learning graph that the chapter teaches.
    3. Prerequisites: the earlier chapters it builds on.
    4. A welcome from Sage: the chapter's goal in a sentence or two.
    5. Main content: explanations, tables, code, and embedded MicroSims.
    6. A recap, usually titled "Putting It All Together", with a list of key takeaways.
    7. Self-check questions: two or more questions whose answers appear when clicked.
    8. A celebration from Sage, then a one-line preview of the next chapter.

    Below each MicroSim is a collapsed specification block. It lists the MicroSim's Bloom's Taxonomy level, learning objective, common misconceptions, and how a student shows mastery. These blocks are useful for lesson planning. Students can skip them.

    Suggested Classroom Use¶

    • Before class: assign the reading. Ask students to try each MicroSim as they reach it.
    • During class: put a MicroSim on the projector. Ask students to predict what a control will do before you change it, then test the prediction. Then move to the kits for the hands-on work.
    • After class: use the self-check questions at the end of the chapter as an exit ticket or a review.
    • Pacing: the chapters run from about 4,700 to 11,300 words, and most mix reading with hands-on work. As a planning estimate, allow about one week per chapter, which fits the book into one semester. Chapters 9, 10, 16 and 18 are the longest and may need more time.

    Using the Kit Labs¶

    The Study Buddy Base Kit section holds 19 short labs that run on the kit. They work alongside the chapters.

    • Part 1, labs 01–07 ("Does It Work?") has one test per part. Students wire one part, run its test, and only then add the next part. This finds wiring mistakes while there is only one place to look.
    • Part 2, labs 08–19 ("How Does It Work?") teaches one idea per lab, from how the display mixes color to a memory game that uses the whole kit. Each lab says what a pass looks like and ends with "Try This" changes.

    The table below suggests where each lab fits. It is a suggestion based on each lab's topic, not a requirement.

    Labs Topic Fits with
    01–07 Wiring and testing each part Chapter 4
    08–10 Color, angles to pixels, flicker-free text Chapters 5–6
    11–13 Button bounce, one loop doing many jobs, state machines Chapter 7
    14–15 How samples make sound, envelopes Chapters 3 and 8
    16–17 Microphone oscilloscope, whistle tuner Chapters 8–11
    18 Wi-Fi, IP, time, HTTP and JSON Chapter 13
    19 Pattern memory game Any time after Chapter 7

    Tips for Running the Labs¶

    • Work in pairs. One student wires while the other checks each wire against the pin table, then they swap.
    • Keep the pin table on paper. Most problems are a wire in the wrong row of the breadboard. The Kit Wiring Fault Finder MicroSim practises tracing these faults.
    • Run lab 02, the hardware probe, whenever something stops working. It checks the parts it can see and reports wiring that breaks the rules.
    • Plan for sound. Labs 05 and 07 and several later labs play sound. Run the quiet labs first, or provide earbuds or set a low volume if many kits share one room.

    Using the MicroSims¶

    What Is a MicroSim?¶

    A MicroSim (short for micro-simulation) is a small interactive simulation that runs in a web browser. Students change a value with a slider, button or menu and see the result at once. MicroSims work on any device with a current browser, with nothing to install.

    MicroSims in This Book¶

    The book has 45 MicroSims. Chapters embed them in iframes, which show one web page inside another, so they load with the chapter. The full list is on the MicroSims page.

    Technology What it is good for Examples
    p5.js (41 MicroSims) Animations and controls students can change Sampling Explorer, FFT Bin Calculator, RGB565 Color Mixer
    vis-network (3 MicroSims) Diagrams of connected parts Study Buddy Architecture Explorer, Kit Import Map, Learning Graph Viewer

    The Colored Dots in the Navigation¶

    Each MicroSim has a colored dot beside its name in the left navigation:

    Dot Status Meaning
    Red Scaffold The design is written but the MicroSim is not built yet
    Orange Built The MicroSim works but the author has not reviewed it yet
    Green Approved The author has tested it and approved it for students

    Most MicroSims are orange right now. Try an orange MicroSim yourself before you rely on it in a lesson, and report anything that looks wrong (see Feedback).

    Tips for Using MicroSims in Class¶

    1. Project them. Ask the class to predict what will happen before you move a control.
    2. Then let students explore. Give them 5–10 minutes on their own devices.
    3. Connect back to the text. Each MicroSim sits next to the idea it shows. After using it, have students reread the paragraph above it.
    4. Plan for the internet. MicroSims need a connection unless you run a local copy of the book (see Customizing Your Own Copy).

    Put a MicroSim on any web page

    You can show any MicroSim on a page you control, such as a Google Site, Canvas, Schoology or a plain HTML file, by pasting this HTML:

    <iframe src="https://dmccreary.github.io/open-study-buddy/sims/kit-wiring-fault-finder/main.html"
    width="100%" height="622px" scrolling="no"></iframe>

    Replace kit-wiring-fault-finder with the name of any MicroSim from the MicroSims page. Each MicroSim's own page shows the right height.

    Using the Glossary¶

    The glossary defines all 326 concepts in the book in alphabetical order. Each definition is short, precise, and does not use the term to define itself.

    • Find a term with Ctrl+F (Cmd+F on a Mac) on the glossary page, or with the search bar at the top of any page.
    • Preview vocabulary by having students look up a chapter's concepts before reading it.
    • Ask students to write their own definitions after a chapter, then compare them with the glossary.

    Using the FAQ¶

    The FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions) answers 90 questions students are likely to ask. The questions are grouped into six categories: Getting Started, Core Concepts, Technical Details, Common Challenges, Best Practices, and Advanced Topics. Each answer links to the chapter that covers the idea.

    • Start a discussion with two or three FAQ questions before you show the answers.
    • Send students to the Common Challenges questions when they are stuck outside class.
    • Use it to review before an assessment.

    Assessment¶

    The book does not have separate quiz pages yet. It offers three kinds of assessment:

    • Self-check questions at the end of every chapter. Students click a question to reveal its answer. They work as exit tickets or warm-ups.
    • Lab pass conditions. Every kit lab says what a pass looks like, so a pass is a quick hands-on check you can sign off.
    • Capstone projects in Chapter 18. Chapter 18 gives four starter projects and a 20-point rubric with six criteria: function, calibration, reliability, privacy, user testing, and code and notes. It also gives a user-testing method in which students watch three or more classmates use their device.

    The MicroSim specification blocks give each MicroSim's Bloom's Taxonomy level. Bloom's Taxonomy sorts thinking skills from simplest to most complex: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate and Create. Use these levels to choose activities that match your goals.

    A Listening Device in Your Classroom¶

    The Study Buddy has a microphone, so plan how you will handle privacy before the microphone chapters. Chapter 17 teaches students to do this, and the same ideas apply to you.

    • Push-to-talk is the default. The device listens only while a student holds a button.
    • Always-on listening needs approval. The wake-word mode ("Hey Pico") listens all the time. The book treats it as an advanced option that should stay off until a parent or guardian, the student and the school have each agreed in writing. Chapter 17 explains who approves what and what records to keep.
    • Do not store audio. The capstone rubric gives points for a device that keeps no recordings, and for one that tells people what it hears and keeps.
    • Use the synthetic schools for demos. The Schools section includes Cedar Ridge High School, an invented school with its own mascot and calendar. Demos can use it and never need real student or school data.
    • Talk to your school first. Ask your school's technology or privacy staff before any device sends audio off the device, even on the school network.

    This guide explains ideas, not the law. Your school's own policies and staff decide what is allowed.

    Customizing the Buddy for Your School¶

    Chapter 16 sets up the agent for one school: the school name, its mascot on the round display, its voice, a custom wake phrase, and a calendar with TEST: and DUE: titles that become spoken reminders. The Schools section shows finished examples and holds pages for real organizations that are piloting the kit. To add your school, contact the author through the contact page.

    The Learning Graph¶

    A learning graph is a map of how the book's concepts depend on each other. It is a directed acyclic graph (DAG): each arrow points from a concept to one that builds on it, and no chain of arrows loops back to where it started. This book's graph has 326 concepts.

    • Check prerequisites: before you teach a concept, see which concepts it needs.
    • Find gaps: when a student is stuck, trace back to the earlier concepts they need.
    • Compare with your syllabus: see which concepts your course already covers.
    • Extend: let advanced students follow the graph ahead of the class.

    Open the Learning Graph Viewer to explore it.

    Sage the Owl: The Book's Guide Character¶

    A pedagogical agent is a character who guides students through a textbook. Research has found that such characters can raise students' interest and how much they feel they learned; this is called the persona effect. This book's character is Sage the Owl, whose catchphrase is "Let's listen closely!"

    Sage appears in colored callout boxes called admonitions:

    Type Purpose How often
    Welcome Introduces the chapter's goal Once, at the start of each chapter
    Thinking Points out a key idea A few times per chapter
    Tip Gives practical advice Where the material calls for it
    Warning Warns about a common mistake Where the material calls for it
    Encourage Supports students on a hard idea Where students may struggle
    Celebration Marks what the chapter achieved Once, at the end of each chapter

    Sage appears a handful of times in each chapter, never twice in a row.

    • Read Sage's boxes aloud. They are written to be spoken.
    • Use the Thinking boxes as discussion prompts. They mark the ideas that matter most.
    • Point frustrated students to the Encourage boxes. They are placed where the material gets hard.

    Feedback¶

    The book is developed in the open on GitHub, a website where people build software and documents together. You do not need to program to report a problem.

    A GitHub issue is like a support ticket: a numbered report that the author and others can discuss. To open one:

    1. Go to the book's GitHub repository.
    2. Click the Issues tab, then New issue.
    3. Give it a clear title, such as "Lab 06 meter does not move" or "Broken link in Chapter 13".
    4. Say which page or lab, what you expected, what happened, and your browser or kit if it matters.
    5. Click Submit new issue.

    Opening an issue needs a free GitHub account. If you would rather not make one, use the contact page. Reports of typos, broken links, MicroSims that misbehave, labs that fail on real hardware, and pages that are hard to read for students with disabilities are all welcome.

    Customizing Your Own Copy¶

    You can make your own version of the book, with your own examples, colors and school.

    Key Terms¶

    • Repository (repo): the folder on GitHub that holds all of a project's files.
    • Git: a tool that records every change to the files, when and by whom.
    • Fork: your own copy of a repository, kept in your GitHub account.
    • Clone: a copy of a repository downloaded to your computer.
    • Markdown: a simple text format. **bold** makes bold, # Heading makes a heading, and a line starting with - makes a bullet point.
    • MkDocs: the program that turns the book's Markdown files into a website.
    • mkdocs.yml: the settings file for the website: title, menu, colors and features.

    Step 1: Copy the Book¶

    Make a free account at github.com if you do not have one. Then either:

    • Fork it (easiest): open the repository and click Fork at the top right, or
    • Clone it (to work on your computer): install Git, open a terminal, and run:
    git clone https://github.com/dmccreary/open-study-buddy.git

    The kit's MicroPython code is in the src/kits/study-buddy-base folder. The book's pages are Markdown files in the docs folder, and you can edit them in any text editor.

    Step 2: Change the Title, Colors and Logo¶

    Open mkdocs.yml. The title and author are near the top:

    site_name: 'Open Study Buddy'
site_description: 'An interactive intelligent textbook to support the low-cost Open Study Buddy personal agent hardware for students.'
site_author: 'Dan McCreary'

    The colors are in the palette section. This book uses amber and orange:

        primary: 'amber'    # try blue, red, purple, teal, green, indigo...
    accent: 'orange'

    The logo in the top-left corner is docs/img/mascot/neutral.png, set by the logo: line in mkdocs.yml. Point that line at your own image to change it.

    Step 3: Preview Your Changes¶

    1. Install Python 3.8 or newer from python.org.

    2. Install MkDocs with the Material theme:

      pip install mkdocs-material

    3. In a terminal, go to the project folder and start the preview:

      cd open-study-buddy
mkdocs serve

    4. Open http://127.0.0.1:8000/open-study-buddy/ in your browser. The page reloads each time you save a file.

    Step 4: Publish Your Version¶

    To publish your copy as a free website with GitHub Pages, run:

    mkdocs gh-deploy

    Your book appears at https://YOUR-USERNAME.github.io/open-study-buddy/ within a few minutes.

    Analytics and Student Data¶

    Web analytics measures how visitors use a site: which pages they read, for how long, and on what devices. It can show which chapters students skip or spend the longest on.

    Adding Google Analytics to Your Copy¶

    This book does not have Google Analytics turned on. To add it to your own copy:

    1. Sign in at analytics.google.com and create a property, Google's name for a site it tracks.
    2. Google gives you a Measurement ID that looks like G-XXXXXXXXXX.

    3. In mkdocs.yml, find the analytics lines near the end, remove the # at the start of each, and put in your ID:

      extra:
  analytics:
    provider: google
    property: G-YOUR-MEASUREMENT-ID

    4. Publish again. Data starts appearing within a day or two.

    Google Analytics reports totals, such as page views and devices. It does not report what each named student did.

    Tracking Individual Students (Advanced)¶

    xAPI (the Experience API) is a standard for recording detailed learning events, such as "this student moved this slider" or "this student passed lab 06". The records are kept in an LRS (Learning Record Store), a database built for learning records.

    Before you collect data about individual students, check these laws:

    • FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act): the U.S. law that protects student education records.
    • COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act): the U.S. law with extra rules for children under 13.
    • State laws: many U.S. states have their own student-privacy laws.
    • GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation): the European Union law that applies to students in the EU.

    Anonymous totals are the safest choice. If you want to track individual students, talk to your school or district's data privacy officer first.

    Purchasing Guide¶

    The whole book runs on one kit, the Study Buddy base kit: a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 WH, a 2.1-inch 360×360 round color display (GC9B72), an INMP441 I²S microphone, a MAX98357A I²S amplifier with a small speaker, three push buttons, a breadboard and wiring. One kit costs about $28 at single-unit prices and about $21 per student for a class of 20. Every part except the Pico ships from China in 8–12 days (median 9) in our 2026 AliExpress orders, so order at least three weeks before the hardware chapters start, and four to five weeks ahead for a full classroom set.

    Prices checked October 2026

    Component prices move, and marketplace listings turn over constantly. Treat every figure here as a planning estimate, not a quote. Each part shows the low and high AliExpress prices we found in web searches on October 20, 2026, with eBay and Amazon prices for comparison. Figures marked estimate could not be confirmed from a current listing, and each one says why.

    Don't order the wrong one: a 360×360 SPI display with the GC9B72 controller

    The kit's driver (lib/gc9b72.py) only works with the 2.1-inch, 360×360, 4-wire SPI round display built on the GC9B72 controller. Marketplaces list 2.1-inch round 480×480 panels (ST7701, RGB or MIPI interface) and 1.28-inch GC9A01 round displays right beside it, with nearly identical photos. Neither will work. Look for "GC9B72" and "360x360" in the listing title.

    Raspberry Pi Pico 2 WH¶

    Shown: the original Pico W, which looks nearly identical to the Pico 2 W. Photo by SparkFun Electronics, CC BY 2.0.

    Used in: Study Buddy base kit (×1)

    Used for: Chapter 4: The Pico Kit Hardware and Wiring and every chapter after it, including Wi-Fi in Chapter 13: Wi-Fi, HTTP, and Connecting to the Agent. It runs the student's MicroPython and talks to every other part.

    The Pico 2 W is Raspberry Pi's RP2350 microcontroller board with 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Buy the WH version, which has its header pins already soldered so it drops straight into the breadboard. The plain "Pico 2 W" has bare pads.

    Requirements: It must be a Pico 2 W (RP2350). The older Pico W (RP2040) and the plain Pico 2 (no Wi-Fi) look almost the same in listing photos. The book's audio, FFT and Wi-Fi chapters assume the RP2350's 520 KB of RAM and the wireless chip. The radio is 2.4 GHz only, so confirm with your school's IT team that a 2.4 GHz network is available.

    Prep work: None for the WH. A plain Pico 2 W needs its headers soldered on (about 15 minutes).

    Cost: ~$6.99 each in quantity 1 (Micro Center "Pico 2W with Header", SC1634) · ~$6.99 each in a classroom order of 20+ · ~$6.99 at district scale (estimate: we assume the fixed retail price holds).     2.1-inch Round Display (GC9B72, 360x360)¶

    Used in: Study Buddy base kit (×1)

    Used for: Chapter 5: Display Basics: Pixels, Color, and Frame Size and Chapter 6: Drawing Directly to Display Regions, then the face, text and level meter in every later chapter.

    A 2.1-inch round IPS color screen with 360×360 pixels and a GC9B72 controller, driven over 4-wire SPI at 3.3 V. The module has a 10-pin header (GND, VCC, SCL, SDA, RST, DC, CS, BL, SDO, TE). The kit uses the first eight. GalaxyCore has never published a GC9B72 datasheet, so the book's driver was ported from the one known-good public init sequence. That makes the exact controller matter more than usual.

    Requirements: It must say GC9B72, 360x360 and SPI. The 480×480 2.1-inch round panels need an RGB or MIPI interface that the Pico does not have.

    Prep work: None. The header comes soldered. Peel off an 8-wire strip of the ribbon cable for it (see below).

    Cost: ~$6.12 in quantity 1 (the AliExpress listing we bought from, US$6.12) · ~$5.39 each in a classroom order of 20+ (the lowest current AliExpress search price) · no district tier found. Price range: AliExpress $5.39–$6.12 (TZT's own store lists it at $3.45); eBay ~$8–$15 (estimate: we could not load current US eBay results); Amazon: no matching 360×360 GC9B72 listing found, only the 480×480 panels that will not work. A European marketplace lists it at €14.64 plus shipping.

    AliExpress lead time: 8–12 days (median 9), the same as our other AliExpress packages. The glass panel ships in a bigger padded box, and a cracked screen means reordering and another 9 days, so buy one or two spares.

    Spotting a bad batch: A blank screen with the backlight on usually means a wiring mistake, so run test 04 first. A panel with a dead line or a dark sector after a correct test is a defect. Set it aside and use a spare.

    INMP441 I2S Microphone¶

    Used in: Study Buddy base kit (×1)

    Used for: Chapter 8: I2S Audio: Recording and Playing Sound, Chapter 11: Microphone Calibration and Push-to-Talk and Chapter 12: Wake-Word Detection. It turns the student's voice into 24-bit samples.

    A digital MEMS microphone with its own ADC, sending samples straight to the Pico over I²S. Buy the INMP441 by name. The "INMP441-compatible" MS3625 and MS4030 boards are cheaper, but the kit's calibration numbers were measured on the INMP441. It is a 3.3 V part: VDD goes to 3V3, never to VBUS.

    Prep work: Some minor soldering required. Most listings ship with the six-pin header loose, as in the photo, and soldering it takes about 5 minutes per board. Listings sold as "solderless" have the header fitted and are worth the extra few cents for a no-solder kit. Tie the L/R pin to GND.

    AliExpress lead time: 8–12 days (median 9).

    MAX98357A I2S Amplifier¶

    Used in: Study Buddy base kit (×1)

    Used for: Chapter 7: Buttons, State Machines, and Device Modes (the volume beep) and Chapter 8: I2S Audio: Recording and Playing Sound, then every chapter where the buddy speaks. It turns I²S audio from the Pico into power for the speaker.

    A digital-to-analog converter and a 3 W class-D amplifier on one small board, with seven pins (LRC, BCLK, DIN, GAIN, SD, GND, VIN) and a two-screw output terminal. The kit drives its GAIN pin from GP12 so software can pick 6, 9 or 12 dB. Buy the mono MAX98357A breakout, not the analog PAM8403 boards or the larger stereo TDM boards that show up in the same searches.

    Prep work: Some minor soldering required on most listings. The seven-pin header and the screw terminal usually ship loose, which takes about 10 minutes per board. Some listings are sold as "pre-soldered" for about the same price, and we recommend those for a no-solder kit.

    Cost: ~$1.53 in quantity 1 · ~$0.99 each in a classroom order of 20+ (most AliExpress listings) · no district tier found. Price range: AliExpress $0.93–$1.53 (pre-soldered listings about $0.99); eBay ~$2–$6 (estimate: we could not load current US eBay results; Adafruit's genuine breakout is $5.95); Amazon $6.88 for a 2-pack, $7.59 for 3, $8.54 for 4 and $11.99 for 6 ($2.00–$3.44 each).

    AliExpress lead time: 8–12 days (median 9).

    Micro-USB Data Cable¶

    Photo: Electronicgrup, CC BY-SA 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons

    Used in: Study Buddy base kit (×1)

    Used for: Chapter 4 and every chapter after it. It powers the kit and carries the Thonny connection.

    The Pico 2 WH has a Micro-USB port, not USB-C. The cable must carry data: charge-only cables look identical and are the most common reason a Pico "isn't detected" on day one. If your laptops have only USB-C ports, buy USB-C to Micro-USB cables. A computer USB port supplies about 500 mA, which is enough for this kit at the volumes the tests use.

    Confirm it says data or sync

    Look for "data", "sync" or "480 Mbps" in the listing. A cable sold only as a "charging cord" may have no data wires at all.

    Prep work: None. Test each cable with a Pico in Thonny on arrival and discard any that only charge.

    Purchasing Strategy¶

    Ordering lead time¶

    These figures are based on our 2026 AliExpress orders to Minnesota: 8 packages, measured from order date to delivery.

    Source Orders measured Typical delivery Notes
    AliExpress 8 packages 8–12 days (median 9) Every 2026 package arrived within two weeks
    Micro Center — same day in store; online "ships in 5–7 business days" The place to buy the Pico 2 WH
    Amazon — 1–3 days with Prime (estimate, not measured) The fallback for cables, buttons and breadboards

    The per-part notes above flag the parts that ship differently: the display (fragile glass in a larger padded box) and the breadboards (bulky, sometimes split into several packages). The kit has no batteries, so no part is held up by lithium shipping rules. If a later version adds a battery pack, expect it to ship separately and take longer.

    Our recommendation: place AliExpress orders at least three weeks before you need the parts, and four to five weeks ahead for a full classroom set. Eight packages is a small sample, and the extra weeks mean a lost package or a cracked display can be reordered and still arrive in time. Use Micro Center or Amazon for last-minute gaps. Expect longer delays around Lunar New Year, when Chinese factories and shipping largely stop for two weeks or more.

    Buy one, then buy twenty. Order a single display, microphone and amplifier from a seller first, confirm they are the GC9B72 360×360 display, a genuine INMP441 and a mono MAX98357A, then place the classroom order with the same sellers.

    Order spares. Add 10–15% over the class count for the display, microphone, amplifier and Pico. Two spares of each add about $30 to a class order.

    Watch the pack sizes. Buttons come in hundreds, breadboards in tens and cables in threes. Round the order up to the pack, not to the roster.

    Buy boards from authorized resellers. Micro Center sells the genuine Pico 2 WH for less than any marketplace listing we found.

    Expect the odd bad unit, not a bad order. Test every part on arrival with the kit's numbered tests (01–07) and discard the occasional dud.

    Check your district's purchasing rules first. Many districts cannot reimburse AliExpress orders and require a domestic vendor with a W-9. If so, the Amazon and Micro Center prices are your real prices. The round display has no Amazon listing we could find, so a district with that rule will need an exception for it or a different domestic seller.
