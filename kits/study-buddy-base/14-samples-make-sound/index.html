    Lab 14: Samples Make Sound¶

    Sound You Can See

    Lab 05 made sound from a list of numbers. This time you will draw every one of those numbers as a dot and hear what happens when a note has too few dots to be drawn properly. One of the notes will sound wrong on purpose. Let's listen closely!
    Program 14-samples-make-sound.py
    Parts needed The speaker, the display, and the buttons
    Time 25 minutes
    Makes sound? Yes. Keep VOLUME low.
    How to stop Press Stop in Thonny, or Ctrl-C

    What You Will Learn¶

    • What a sample is, and what sample rate means
    • How to calculate samples per cycle for any note
    • What the Nyquist limit is
    • What aliasing is, and why a high note can sound like a lower one

    The Big Idea¶

    A speaker cone moves in and out smoothly, but the Pico can only send it a list of numbers, once every 1/16,000th of a second. Each number is one sample: where the cone should be at that instant. A note of frequency f repeats f times a second, so each cycle of the wave gets

    samples per cycle = sample rate / frequency

    At the kit's rate of 16,000 samples a second:

    Frequency Samples per cycle What the dots look like
    110 Hz 145.5 A very smooth, slow curve
    440 Hz 36.4 A smooth wave
    1,760 Hz 9.1 A visible zig-zag
    3,520 Hz 4.5 A rough wave
    7,000 Hz 2.3 Almost a straight zig-zag

    The higher the note, the fewer dots draw each wave.

    The Nyquist limit¶

    The least number of samples that can still describe a wave is 2 per cycle: one up, one down. That happens at half the sample rate, which is 8,000 Hz here. It is called the Nyquist limit.

    Go above it and the samples cannot tell your wave apart from a lower one. The note you hear is the wrong note. This is aliasing. The last step in the program is 10,000 Hz, which is above the limit, and its samples are exactly those of a 6,000 Hz wave (16,000 - 10,000). So it sounds lower than the 7,000 Hz step just before it.

    Same Dots, Two Waves

    Draw a dot on a graph every time a clock ticks, and many different waves pass through the very same dots, the way many roads pass through the same two towns. When two waves fit the same dots, the Pico plays the slower one, and that is why movie wagon wheels seem to spin backwards.

    Many things in the real world use this rule. CD audio uses 44,100 samples a second so it can reproduce notes up to 22,050 Hz, just above what people can hear. The Study Buddy's microphone uses 12,800 samples a second, so it can hear up to 6,400 Hz, which is plenty for speech.

    Walking Through the Code¶

    One function makes every sample¶

    def sample(freq, i, volume=VOLUME):
    return int(32767 * volume / 100 * math.sin(2 * math.pi * freq * i / RATE))

    This is the same formula as Lab 05. Sample number i happens at time i / RATE seconds, and the sine of the angle gives the cone position. Both drawing and playing use this one function, so the dots on the screen are exactly the numbers sent to the speaker.

    Drawing the dots¶

    def plot(freq):
    ...
    for i in range(PLOT_SAMPLES):
        x = PLOT_X + i * PLOT_W // PLOT_SAMPLES
        y = PLOT_MID - sample(freq, i, 100) * PLOT_HALF // 32767
        display.fill_rect(x, y - 1, DOT, DOT, config.CYAN)

    The plot shows the first 72 samples, one dot each, spread across a 280-pixel box. A sample of 32767 is the top of the box and -32767 is the bottom, so * PLOT_HALF // 32767 scales the number to 52 pixels either way. Because the box shows only 72 samples, a low note shows only part of one cycle, and a high note shows many.

    Playing the note¶

    def play(freq):
    count = RATE * NOTE_MS // 1000
    fade = RATE // 100
    buf = bytearray(count * 2)
    for i in range(count):
        level = min(1.0, i / fade, (count - i) / fade)
        struct.pack_into("<h", buf, i * 2, int(sample(freq, i) * level))
    audio.write(buf)

    The note is 600 ms long (9,600 samples). The fade is the 10 ms ramp from Lab 05 that stops clicks at the start and end.

    The buttons¶

    Button Does
    UP Next, higher frequency
    DOWN Previous, lower frequency
    MODE Plays the same one again

    Each press is an edge (a 1 to 0 change, as in Lab 11), and calls show(index), which updates the text, draws the dots, and plays the note. The index is kept in range with min and max:

    index = min(len(FREQS) - 1, index + 1)     # stops at the last item
index = max(0, index - 1)                  # stops at the first

    Warning the listener¶

    if freq > RATE // 2:
    put("past Nyquist: heard as %d Hz" % (RATE - freq), 252, config.RED)
elif per_cycle < 4:
    put("close to Nyquist", 252, config.color565(255, 140, 0))

    The screen shows close to Nyquist in orange when a note has fewer than 4 samples per cycle, and past Nyquist: heard as 6000 Hz in red when it is over the limit.

    Run It¶

    Open 14-samples-make-sound.py and press Run. It starts with 440 Hz. Press UP to climb through the notes, and watch the dots and listen to the pitch.

    What PASS Looks Like¶

    • Each press plays a note and redraws the dots.
    • Low notes are smooth curves of many dots. High notes are a few dots in a zig-zag.
    • The screen shows the samples per cycle, such as 36.4 samples per cycle for 440 Hz.
    • 10000 Hz sounds lower than 7000 Hz, and the screen says why.

    If Something Goes Wrong¶

    What you hear or see Likely cause Fix
    No sound Speaker wiring Run Lab 05 first
    The 10000 Hz note does not sound lower than 7000 Hz You may have missed the change Play 7000 Hz and then 10000 Hz again, and listen for the drop in pitch
    Very high notes are hard to hear Small speakers are weak at high pitch Hold the speaker closer, with VOLUME still low
    Dots do not move Buttons not wired Run Lab 03

    Try This¶

    1. Slower sampling. Change RATE to 8000 (the I2S rate follows it). Which notes are now above Nyquist? Does 440 Hz sound any different?
    2. Prove the alias. Add 9000 to FREQS. It should sound exactly like 7,000 Hz. Print a few samples of each: how are the two lists related?
    3. A square wave. Replace math.sin with a square wave: 1 if (freq * i / RATE) % 1 < 0.5 else -1. How do the dots change? How does the sound change?
    4. Find your highest hearing. Add 12,000, 14,000, and 15,000 to FREQS. What does each alias to? Which can you actually hear?
    Hint for number 2

    9,000 Hz is above Nyquist, and 16,000 - 9,000 = 7,000. The samples of 9,000 Hz are the negatives of those of 7,000 Hz, the same wave flipped upside down. Your ear cannot tell a flipped wave from the original, so they sound the same.

    Hint for number 3

    A square wave has only two values, so the dots sit in two rows: top and bottom. A square wave sounds buzzy and bright compared to the smooth sine, because it contains many extra high-pitched parts.

    Check Your Understanding¶

    1. How many samples per cycle does a 1,000 Hz note have at 16,000 samples a second?
    2. What is the Nyquist limit when the sample rate is 12,800 Hz?
    3. A 9,000 Hz note is played with a 16,000 Hz sample rate. What pitch do you hear?
    Answers
    1. 16,000 / 1,000 = 16 samples per cycle.
    2. Half of 12,800, which is 6,400 Hz.
    3. 7,000 Hz (16,000 - 9,000), because 9,000 is above the Nyquist limit of 8,000.
